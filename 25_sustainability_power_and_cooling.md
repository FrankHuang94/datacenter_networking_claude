# Power, Cooling, and Sustainability in Datacenter Networking

## Introduction: The Constraint That Bounds Everything

Power is the ultimate constraint on datacenter networking. Every bit moved costs energy; every switch, transceiver, and NIC dissipates heat that must be removed; and the aggregate power draw of the AI datacenter has grown so large that it strains regional electrical grids and shapes where datacenters can be built at all. Networking is a meaningful and growing fraction of that power, and the energy-per-bit of interconnect technology is one of the most important figures of merit in the entire field — the reason co-packaged optics exists, the reason chiplets are integrated, the reason the interconnect hierarchy is flattening. This chapter examines power, cooling, and sustainability as they pertain to datacenter networking: networking's share of datacenter power, the AI-driven escalation, the savings from CPO/LPO, liquid cooling for high-power switches, power-delivery efficiency, the carbon footprint of optical networking, and the efficiency metrics that increasingly govern design. It closes the database by grounding all the preceding technology in the physical constraint that bounds it.

## Networking's Share of Datacenter Power

In a typical datacenter, servers consume the majority of IT power (~70%), networking a meaningful slice (~10–15%), and cooling and power-delivery overhead the rest (captured in the PUE, Power Usage Effectiveness, metric). The networking share, while smaller than compute, is substantial in absolute terms: a 400G switch draws on the order of 400 W, its transceivers another ~2 kW (4 W × ~512 ports), and the NICs across a rack of servers tens of kilowatts. As fabrics scale to 800G and beyond, and as AI fabrics demand non-blocking, high-radix topologies with far more switches and transceivers per GPU, networking's absolute power grows even if its percentage share holds.

## AI Networking Power Escalation

AI clusters escalate networking power on every axis. An **NVIDIA DGX H100** draws ~5.6 kW for its eight H100 GPUs (700 W each), and its networking adds up: four ConnectX-7 NICs (~80 W each = 320 W), a shared portion of the InfiniBand switch power, and — significantly — the **transceiver power** (400G optics at ~2–4 W each, multiplied across many ports per NIC, can reach hundreds of watts per system). While networking is perhaps ~6% of a DGX's total power, the transceiver power in particular adds up across a cluster, and at the switch the optics dominate: a 51.2 Tbps switch's pluggable transceivers can draw more than the switch ASIC itself (File 13). At cluster scale — tens of thousands of GPUs, with all their NICs, switches, and optics — networking power is measured in megawatts, a first-order line item in the datacenter's power budget and a direct driver of the optical-integration transition.

## CPO and LPO Power Savings

The single largest networking-power lever is the optical-integration transition (File 13). **Co-Packaged Optics (CPO)** saves the **3–5 pJ/bit** that electrical SerDes spend driving signals from the ASIC to front-panel pluggables. At 51.2 Tbps, 3 pJ/bit × 51.2×10¹² bits/s ≈ **150 W saved per switch** — and across 1,000 switches in a datacenter, **~150 kW saved**, a major reduction. **LPO (Linear Drive Optics)** captures a meaningful fraction of this nearer-term by removing the module DSP (~2–4 W per 400G transceiver, ~1 kW per 256-port switch). These savings are the fundamental justification for the enormous engineering effort behind CPO and LPO: at hyperscale, the power saved translates directly into capital and operating cost, into reduced cooling load, and into the ability to fit more compute within a fixed power envelope.

## Liquid Cooling for Networking

Networking equipment has traditionally been air-cooled, but the highest-power AI switches and ASICs (Tomahawk-class at 800 W+, plus co-packaged optics) are pushing networking toward **liquid cooling**, following the GPUs. Options include **Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC)** (cold plates on the hot components), **rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx)** (liquid-cooled doors on the rack absorbing exhaust heat), and **immersion cooling** (submerging equipment in dielectric fluid). Vendors (Dell, HPE, Vertiv, and others) offer DLC for networking, but the challenges are real: plumbing liquid to switches, integrating with the rack's cooling infrastructure, and managing the reliability of liquid near electronics and — for CPO — near delicate optical components. As switch and CPO power density rises, liquid cooling for networking, once exotic, is becoming necessary, mirroring the broader datacenter shift to liquid cooling driven by AI compute.

## Power Delivery and Conversion Efficiency

Power-delivery efficiency is an underappreciated lever. A switch ASIC runs on low-voltage rails (e.g., ~0.7 V core, 1.8 V I/O), and converting from the datacenter's distribution voltage down to these rails incurs losses at each stage. **Google's adoption of 48 V distribution** directly to the board (versus the legacy 12 V) reduces conversion losses (lower current for the same power means lower I²R loss). For **CPO**, power delivery is more complex still, with the photonic engine needing its own rails (~3.3 V) alongside the ASIC's sub-volt rails on the same package. Optimizing the power-delivery network — fewer conversion stages, higher distribution voltage, efficient voltage regulators — saves energy across the entire fleet and is an active area of datacenter and OCP design.

## Carbon Footprint of Optical Networking

Sustainability extends beyond operational power to the full lifecycle. The **carbon footprint** of optical networking includes the energy to manufacture transceivers and fiber, the embodied carbon of the equipment, and the 24/7 operational power of always-on infrastructure — notably **EDFAs** running continuously in subsea and long-haul systems, and the optical PoPs that must be powered and cooled. Hyperscalers increasingly power their datacenters (and optical infrastructure) with **renewable energy** and account for **Scope 3 emissions** from their hardware supply chains, including networking equipment. The carbon cost of the networking supply chain — semiconductor fabrication, compound-semiconductor (InP, GaAs) processing, module assembly — is a growing consideration as the industry confronts its total environmental impact.

## Network Efficiency Metrics

The field increasingly governs design by **efficiency metrics**:
- **Bits per Joule** at the switch ASIC and at the transceiver — improving roughly 1.5× per generation through process scaling, architectural improvements, and the optical-integration transition.
- **Total network energy per useful transaction** — the energy to deliver an email, an AI inference, or a video stream — a holistic measure of network efficiency.
- **Green Grid metrics** such as PUE and proposed IT-level efficiency metrics (ITUE) that attempt to capture how efficiently the infrastructure converts power into useful work.

These metrics make energy a first-class design criterion alongside bandwidth and latency, and they increasingly determine which technologies win: a fabric that delivers the same bandwidth at lower energy per bit is not just cheaper to run but enables more compute within a power-constrained datacenter.

## Energy-Proportional Networking

An aspiration — only partially realized — is **energy-proportional networking**, where power consumption scales with utilization rather than remaining constant. **Energy Efficient Ethernet (IEEE 802.3az)** reduces power on idle enterprise links, but it has **no datacenter equivalent**, because lossless AI/HPC fabrics need their links always on and ready — a paused or low-power link would add latency to the next collective. The tension between energy proportionality and the always-on requirement of lossless fabrics is a real research challenge: ideas include partial-rate operation (a 400G link running at a lower data rate but, problematically, often not at proportionally lower power) and adaptive bandwidth switching, but no clean solution exists for the lossless datacenter fabric. PCIe's L0p dynamic lane-width reduction (File 03) is one of the few examples of true energy proportionality in datacenter interconnect, and extending such ideas to the fabric is an open problem.

## Extended Deep Dive: The Datacenter Power Crisis and the Grid

The escalation of AI-datacenter power has reached a scale that strains the **electrical grid itself**, and this macro context frames why networking power efficiency matters beyond the individual datacenter. A single large AI training campus can demand hundreds of megawatts — comparable to a small city — and the aggregate growth of AI datacenter power has become a significant factor in regional and national electricity planning, driving utilities to build new generation and transmission, prompting datacenter operators to secure dedicated power (including nuclear and renewable contracts), and in some regions creating power availability, not capital or chips, as the binding constraint on AI buildout. When power is the limiting resource, every watt spent inefficiently — including networking watts spent driving signals across lossy copper to front-panel pluggables (File 13) — directly reduces the compute that can be deployed within the available power envelope.

This reframes networking power efficiency as not merely a cost-saving measure but a **capacity enabler**: in a power-constrained datacenter, reducing networking power (via co-packaged optics, LPO, more efficient switch silicon, better power delivery) frees power for more GPUs, more useful work. The 150 kW saved across a datacenter's switches by CPO (above) is not just a lower electricity bill; it is power that can instead run additional accelerators. As the grid constraint tightens, the pressure on every component — compute, memory, and networking — to deliver more useful work per watt intensifies, and the energy-per-bit improvements that the optical-integration transition promises become strategically essential, not merely economically attractive. The datacenter power crisis is the macro context that elevates networking energy efficiency from an engineering nicety to a determinant of how much AI capability can be built at all.

## Extended Deep Dive: Liquid Cooling and the Co-Packaged-Optics Thermal Coupling

The shift to liquid cooling (above) interacts with the co-packaged-optics transition (File 13) in ways worth drawing out, because the two trends are coupled. As switch ASICs and co-packaged optical engines share a package dissipating up to ~1000 W, air cooling becomes infeasible and direct liquid cooling becomes necessary — but the co-packaged optics introduce a complication absent from pure-compute liquid cooling: the optical engine's temperature-sensitive components (microring resonators shifting ~0.08 nm/°C, File 13) must be held in a narrow temperature window even as the adjacent ASIC dumps enormous heat. Liquid cooling, by providing a high-capacity, controllable heat removal path, is part of the solution to the CPO thermal problem — it can hold the package at a stable temperature more effectively than air — but the cooling design must manage the thermal gradient between the hot ASIC and the temperature-sensitive optics, potentially with separate cooling zones or careful thermal-interface design.

This coupling means that the liquid-cooling infrastructure, the switch-ASIC thermal design, and the co-packaged-optics thermal management must be **co-designed** — yet another instance of the cross-domain co-design that recurs throughout this database. The broader datacenter implication is that the move to liquid cooling (already underway for GPUs) extends to networking as switches and CPO push past air-cooling limits, requiring liquid-cooling plumbing, manifolds, and heat-rejection infrastructure throughout the datacenter, not just at the GPU racks. The thermal and cooling architecture thus becomes a shared constraint binding compute and networking together, and the efficiency of heat removal (which itself consumes power, captured in PUE) is part of the total energy equation. Power, cooling, and the optical-integration transition are facets of a single thermal-energy challenge that increasingly governs datacenter design.

## Extended Deep Dive: Embodied Carbon and the Full Lifecycle

While operational power dominates the sustainability conversation, the **embodied carbon** of networking hardware — the emissions from manufacturing, transporting, and eventually disposing of it — is a growing and often-overlooked concern that deserves treatment. Manufacturing a switch ASIC, an optical transceiver, or a fiber cable consumes substantial energy and materials: semiconductor fabrication is energy- and water-intensive, compound-semiconductor processing (InP, GaAs for lasers and modulators) involves toxic materials and energy-intensive processes, and the rare and refined materials in optics and electronics carry their own extraction footprint. As operators decarbonize their operational power (renewable energy for the datacenter), the embodied carbon of the hardware becomes a larger relative fraction of the total footprint, and Scope 3 emissions (those in the supply chain, outside the operator's direct control) come under scrutiny.

This has several implications for networking. The **longevity and reuse** of equipment matters — hardware that lasts longer or can be repurposed amortizes its embodied carbon over more useful work, arguing against premature forklift upgrades (and, conversely, the rapid generational churn of AI-driven networking, where 400G is replaced by 800G by 1.6T in a few years, raises the embodied-carbon cost of the buildout). The **efficiency of manufacturing** (semiconductor fabs powered by renewables, less wasteful processes) reduces the embodied footprint. And the **end-of-life** handling (recycling the materials, especially the valuable and toxic compound semiconductors and the rare materials) closes the loop. As the AI buildout drives an enormous wave of new networking hardware — millions of transceivers, vast switch fleets, new fiber — the embodied carbon of that hardware, alongside its operational power, becomes a material part of the technology industry's environmental impact, and a complete sustainability accounting (the focus of this chapter) must include the full lifecycle, not just the electricity bill. The most sustainable networking is not only the most energy-efficient in operation but the most material-efficient and longest-lived across its lifecycle.

## Extended Deep Dive: Cooling, Water, and Datacenter Siting

Cooling deserves a fuller treatment in its sustainability dimension, because it intersects with water use and datacenter siting in ways that increasingly constrain where and how AI infrastructure can be built. Removing the heat that compute and networking generate requires energy (captured in PUE) and, for many cooling approaches, **water** — evaporative cooling, common in large datacenters for its efficiency, consumes significant water, and as AI datacenters grow and as they are sited in water-stressed regions, water use has become a contentious sustainability and community concern. The shift to **liquid cooling** (driven by the rising power density of GPUs and now high-power switches and co-packaged optics, File 25 above) changes the equation: direct liquid cooling can be more energy-efficient than air cooling (liquid carries heat more effectively, reducing the energy spent on air movement), and closed-loop liquid systems can reduce water consumption relative to evaporative cooling — though the heat must still ultimately be rejected to the environment.

These cooling and water constraints, together with the power constraints (File 25 above), increasingly drive **datacenter siting**: operators locate datacenters where power (ideally renewable) is abundant and cheap, where the climate aids cooling (cooler regions reduce cooling energy), and where water is available or where water-efficient cooling is feasible. The AI buildout's enormous power and cooling demands have made siting a strategic constraint — power availability, cooling feasibility, and water access now rank alongside network connectivity and land cost in datacenter location decisions, and they shape the geography of where AI infrastructure can be built at all. The networking implication is that AI clusters may be sited where the power and cooling are, and connected to users and to each other over long-haul and metro DCI (File 12) — reinforcing the importance of the optical interconnect that ties geographically distributed, power-and-cooling-optimized datacenters together. Cooling and its water and siting dimensions are thus not peripheral but central to how the sustainable AI datacenter is built, and they connect the power constraint of this chapter to the DCI and distributed-infrastructure themes of File 12.

## Conclusion

Power is the constraint that bounds everything in datacenter networking, and it has become one of the primary drivers of the field's technology trajectory. Networking's share of datacenter power, modest in percentage but enormous in absolute terms and growing with AI, has made energy-per-bit a first-order figure of merit — the fundamental justification for co-packaged optics, chiplet integration, and the flattening of the interconnect hierarchy. The escalation of AI-fabric power is driving networking toward liquid cooling, more efficient power delivery, and relentless improvement in bits per Joule, while the lifecycle carbon footprint and the (largely unmet) goal of energy-proportional networking pose challenges that the field is only beginning to address. As AI datacenters grow to consume the output of entire power plants, and as grids strain to supply them, the efficiency of the network — not just its bandwidth and latency — increasingly determines what is possible. The technologies surveyed throughout this database will ultimately be judged not only by how fast they move bits, but by how little energy they spend doing it. Power is where the abstract promise of the network meets the hard physical limits of the world it runs in, and managing that constraint is the defining challenge of the datacenter's next decade.
